# The 1940s CO₂ plateau — literature, and what it does to our mid-century problem

Compiled 2026-08-23 on Marcus's recollection that the 1940s pause is a known, unexplained
feature. **The recollection is correct, and it re-poses the project's mid-century question.**

---

## 1. What the literature says

**The feature.** Atmospheric CO₂ stalls at ~310–312 ppm from ~1940 to ~1955 in the Law Dome
record — a "plateau" during a period of continued and rising fossil emissions. It **persists in
the revised Rubino et al. (2019) record**, which is the exact dataset this repo scores against
(`law2018co2.txt`, MANIFEST key `lawdome`).

**The implied sink.** ~**2–3 GtC/yr uptake between 1942 and 1949** is required to produce it.
**That number is computed AFTER accounting for firn diffusion and bubble trapping** — the
smoothing correction makes the required sink *larger*, not smaller. This matters here: it means
the plateau is not disposed of as a firn artifact, and the correction runs against the direction
an artifact explanation needs.

**Attribution.** Rubino et al. (2019) state the δ¹³C-CO₂ measurements indicate **"the oceans were
responsible for at least two-thirds of this uptake"**; other framings of the same analysis put it
at "mostly oceanic, but could have been up to one third biospheric" around 1945–1948.

**It is not explained.** Bastos et al. (2016), *Re-evaluating the 1940s CO₂ plateau*,
Biogeosciences 13:4877, put the model–observation mismatch over 1940–1950 at **0.9–2.0 PgC/yr**
and conclude **"the previously proposed ocean hypothesis for the 1940s plateau cannot be confirmed
by independent data."** They question the 1940–42 El Niño explanation because modern ocean CO₂
data show only modest ocean-sink variability; ocean models supply only ~0.5 PgC/yr of the needed
uptake. Rubino et al.'s own hedge: recent modeling studies "were not able to explain the plateau,
although they did not consider decadal variability in the ocean carbon sink that may have been
important." Land-abandonment during WWII has been floated and is not established.

## 2. What it does to this project — four consequences

**(a) Our "mid-century growth-rate pause" IS the published 1940s plateau.** Handoff 2026-08-22 §3a
established the pause is real at **4.7× its own noise floor** and — on the raw estimator, once the
LOESS artifact was removed — sits in the **1940s**, not the 1950s. That is the plateau. We
rediscovered a named feature and were treating it as an open anomaly of our own.

**(b) No model reproduces it, so ours failing to is not a defect unique to us.** Bastos's
0.9–2.0 PgC/yr mismatch is across state-of-the-art reconstructions with the best available
emissions, ocean and land estimates. **This substantially de-ranks "make the model fit the
mid-century" as a target** — it is an open problem in the field, not a bug in this box model.

**(c) Our 12.7 σ isotopic rejection of a terrestrial source AGREES with the published δ¹³C
attribution, and is the stronger statement.** Handoff 2026-08-22 §3c measured the required
terrestrial δ¹³C excursion at −0.209 ‰ against an observed −0.003 ± 0.016 ‰. The literature says
oceans ≥ two-thirds, up to one third biospheric. **Same direction, independently derived.** That
is a real convergence and it raises confidence in that gate — which was mutation-tested at 6.4 se
and had corrected its own transfer ratio by 2.4×.

**(d) It coheres with the GCB budget-imbalance finding.** Handoff 2026-08-22 §3b: our growth-rate
error tracks GCB's published `imbalance` at r = 0.771, and that imbalance peaks at **+1.30 (1940s)
and +1.62 GtC/yr (1950s)** — GCB cannot close its own budget in exactly the plateau era, by
20.9 ppm cumulative over 1930–69. Three independent lines — the ice record, GCB's own residual,
and our model — all break down in the same decade.

## 3. What it does to Marcus's ice-vs-flask challenge (handoff §6b)

Marcus asked whether the ice and flask systems could carry very different offsets, which would
explain the model's trouble across the transition. **Two tests say the OFFSET does not differ, and
the literature says the TRANSITION problem is real anyway — for a different reason.**

- **TEST 1 (done; no download needed — the raw samples were already on disk).** `obs$ice` is the
  RAW Law Dome sample file, not the spline (DATA.md:52 mislabels it; the build script reads
  `law2018co2.txt` through `read_law_samples()`). In the 1962–1996 overlap, ice − SPO =
  **−0.31 ± 0.15 ppm**, and split by sample type: **FIRN −0.40 ± 0.19 (n=20), ICE −0.24 ± 0.23
  (n=27)**, differing from each other by 0.16 ppm (t = −0.53, n.s.). Both types are independent of
  Scripps flasks, so this is a genuine cross-validation. **The circularity caveat in handoff §6b
  is RETRACTED.**
- **TEST 2 (done).** Pre-1958 residual (model − obs), n = 160 samples 1851–1958:
  regressed on **dCO₂/dt: R² 0.209, slope −11.7 ppm per (ppm/yr), p = 1.2e-09**; on **cumulative
  emissions: R² 0.045, p = 0.0069**. Together R² 0.334, predictors correlated only 0.279. **Growth
  rate explains 4.6× more variance.** The slope is NEGATIVE: the model falls further below
  observations when CO₂ rises fast. **Firn smoothing predicts the opposite sign** (it biases ice
  low, making model − obs more positive). A too-old gas-age placement would give this sign, but
  Test 1 bounds placement at ≈0.2 yr equivalent, where explaining ~6 ppm at a pre-1958 growth rate
  of ~0.5 ppm/yr needs ~12 yr — a factor of ~60.

**So: the offset does not change across the transition, but the model's residual does — because
the observations contain a real, unexplained feature there that no model reproduces.** Marcus's
instinct that the ice→flask transition is where the trouble lives was right; the mechanism is the
plateau, not a representation offset.

## 4. Consequence for `rep_dC`

`rep_dC` = 5.57 ppm is the **flask-era** mean offset (measured 5.47, constant to ~1 ppm over
66 yr). The ice-era residual it does *not* cover is dominated by the model's failure to reproduce
a feature the field cannot reproduce either. **So the "+5.57 σ prior violation" is better read as:
the model absorbs its modern level offset into `rep_dC`, and carries its share of a known
open problem in the ice era.** Neither re-centering nor rescaling that prior touches either half.

### 5a. The numbers, from the paper rather than the abstract page

- Plateau: **CO₂ stabilizes at ~310–312 ppm from 1940 to the early 1950s** (Etheridge et al.
  1996; reconfirmed by MacFarling Meure et al. 2006 and Rubino et al. 2013). Bastos's Fig. 1
  shades 1940–1950.
- **Mismatch 0.9–2.0 PgC/yr over 1940–1950**, depending on which of four LUC datasets is used.
- Bastos's own framing of the failure: the reconstructions **"greatly overestimate atmospheric CO₂
  growth rate during the plateau period, as well as in the 1960s"**.
- CMIP5 ocean models: natural variability could supply **an additional 0.5 PgC/yr, "but it is
  unlikely to be higher"**. Brovkin et al. (2004): even with **zero** land-use emissions CO₂ should
  still have risen **2.6 ppm** over 1940–1950.
- Their three candidate causes: (i) decadal ocean-sink variability not in the reconstructions;
  (ii) a further terrestrial sink missing from land-surface models; (iii) **LUC processes not in
  current datasets** — OSCAR v2.2 tests suggest land-use mis-accounting during the WWII
  socioeconomic disruption "could contribute to the additional sink required".
- Verdict: **"the previously proposed ocean hypothesis for the 1940s plateau cannot be confirmed
  by independent data."**

**Our model reproduces the literature's failure mode.** Test 2's slope on dCO₂/dt is negative, so
when observed growth is LOW (the plateau) our model runs above observations — i.e. it overestimates
growth through the plateau, which is exactly Bastos's characterization of the reconstructions.

### 5b. A CAVEAT THAT APPLIES TO OUR 12.7 σ, AND IT IS THE IMPORTANT ONE

§2(c) above claimed our 12.7 σ terrestrial rejection "agrees with the published δ¹³C attribution
and is the stronger statement". **The paper qualifies exactly that inference, so that claim must
be softened until checked.** Bastos, §1:

- **"in spite of the high quality of the Law Dome δ¹³C record, the scatter and uncertainty in the
  data are relatively high and they affect how well it is possible to partition the biospheric and
  oceanic fluxes. Errors in the δ¹³C data may lead to spurious and highly correlated terrestrial
  and oceanic fluxes (Francey et al., 1995)."**
- And on the one published *terrestrial* explanation (Rafelski et al. 2009): when terrestrial
  uptake is used to explain the plateau it produces a δ¹³C peak that "appears to be inconsistent
  with δ¹³C measurements, **although the differences are not large compared to the measurement
  uncertainties** (Trudinger et al., 2002a)."

**That last clause is in direct tension with our 12.7 σ.** Handoff 2026-08-22 §3c required
−0.209 ‰ for a terrestrial explanation against an observed **−0.003 ± 0.016 ‰**. Trudinger et al.
found the same class of discrepancy "not large compared to the measurement uncertainties". Either
we are testing a different quantity, or **our ±0.016 ‰ is too small**. **ACTION: validate that
error bar against the published Law Dome δ¹³C scatter before quoting 12.7 σ again.** It is the
single most load-bearing number the 08-22 session produced.

### 5c. NEW TEST — the air-age spectral width, a within-dataset control

Bastos §2.1 supplies what the repo did not have: Law Dome's air-age distribution has an average
**spectral width of 4.5 yr in DE08 and DE08-2, 5.8 yr in DSS ice, and 7.0 yr in DSSW20K firn**
(Trudinger et al. 2002b). `SampleID` identifies the core, so **if the pre-1958 residual is a
firn/bubble smoothing artifact it must scale with that width.**

| core | width (yr) | n (1850–1958) | mean residual | se |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| DE08 | 4.5 | 125 | −2.46 | 0.21 |
| DE08-2 | 4.5 | 10 | −2.06 | 0.69 |
| DSS | 5.8 | 14 | −2.49 | 0.59 |
| DSSW20K | 7.0 | 2 | −3.11 | 2.98 |

`resid ~ width + yr`: slope **−0.180 ppm per yr-of-width, p = 0.66** (1850–1958, n = 151);
**−0.667, p = 0.053** over 1930–1958 (n = 66). Smoothing an *accelerating* curve makes ice read
LOW, so it predicts a **positive** slope. **The observed slope is null or the wrong sign.**
*Weak test, stated as such:* the widest core (DSSW20K, 7.0 yr) has only 2 samples in the overlap
window and 8 in the file, so the long arm of the lever is barely sampled.

### 5d. CORRECTION TO TEST 2's REASONING (§3 above)

§3 argued firn smoothing "predicts the opposite sign" using **dCO₂/dt**. That is the wrong
predictor: a *symmetric* smoothing kernel introduces **no** bias on a linear ramp — the bias goes
as **curvature × width²**. Re-run with the right predictor:

| predictor | R² | slope | p |
|---|---|---|---|
| dCO₂/dt (growth rate) | **0.212** | −11.82 | 9.2e-10 |
| **d²CO₂/dt² (the smoothing predictor)** | **0.008** | +11.06 | **0.27** |

The two are essentially uncorrelated (r = 0.004), so this is a clean separation. **The smoothing
hypothesis fails on its own correct predictor — a stronger result than §3 had, reached by a
different argument than §3 gave.** The growth-rate dependence is real and is not smoothing; it is
the model lagging when forcing is strongest.
