CarboTope II: CarboTope vs. Bad Science

This is a good time to explore some of the intuitive challenges with the carbon cycle. First, humans have emitted more than 500 gigatons of carbon from fossil fuels since 1750.[FN2] Compared to about 40,000 GtC spread through the atmosphere (590 GtC), land (2,100 GtC), and ocean (the biggest player at 37,000 GtC), that’s 1.3%. That is of the magnitude where it might seem like we could rely on “the solution to pollution is dilution.”

There are two problems with that intuition. The first is the “peculiar buffer mechanism of sea water” identified by Revelle and Suess (1957). When CO2 dissolves in water, it reacts with H2O to form H2CO3. Since the ocean is slightly basic, the carbonic acid wants to donate a proton… and the best way to do that is to react with a carbonate anion (CO32−), leading to the formation of two bicarbonate (HCO3−) molecules. But where did that carbonate come from? Mostly it was already dissolved in seawater, where it is the raw material for calcium carbonate aka aragonite,[FN3] the building blocks for shells and corals. This is why CO2 is bad for corals.[FN4] In any case, every dissolved CO2 molecule needs a carbonate ion to partner with. This means that as carbonate ions get used up, the ability of the ocean to dissolve CO2 declines (Figure 2). The end result of this is that for an increase in atmospheric CO2 concentration of 10%, the ocean increases in equilibrium concentration by about 1% or less (this is called the Revelle factor).[FN5] This property alone changes a 1.3% human addition to total carbon to an equilibrium 14% increase in atmospheric concentration (or 39 ppm).

[INSERT IMAGE: fig2_buffer_chemistry.png]

Figure 2: The peculiar buffer mechanism of sea water.[FN6]

The second problem is the slow rate of deep ocean mixing. Figure 1 shows how long it takes for carbon to make its way into the deep ocean. Equilibration is a slow process. I’ll also note that with increasing emissions, the Revelle factor increases, and with increasing temperature, the rate of ocean mixing slows, which just adds to the lifetime of CO2 in the atmosphere.

With that lesson out of the way, let’s get to the contrarians!

Contrarian Mistakes

Deep doubts, deep wisdom; shallow doubts, shallow wisdom — Chinese proverb cited by Koutsoyiannis et al. 2022

Keep an open mind… but not so open that your brains fall out.[FN7]

There is, I think, some fundamental discomfort with the idea that carbon dioxide emissions are effectively permanent on human timescales. This is particularly acute in the contrarian community where there is pushback to attributing any global trend to human influence, whether concentration or temperature or sea level rise. This has led to a plethora of repeated blogposts and publications pushing short CO2 atmospheric lifetimes. Many derive from three confusions: the first is residence time (how long an individual CO2 molecule would be expected to stay in the atmosphere before moving to the ocean or ecosystem pools) versus perturbation time (how long atmospheric concentrations stay elevated after a CO2 molecule is added)[FN8]; the second is thinking that the relationship between year-to-year temperature swings (due to ENSO) and CO2 means that CO2 concentration changes are primarily driven by temperature; the third is using overly simplistic models calibrated to observations to disprove sophisticated carbon cycle models and understanding.[FN9] Added to these confusions is a tendency of some physicists and engineers to assume they know better (see Figure 3).

[INSERT IMAGE: fig3_xkcd_physicists.png]

Figure 3: Go to the XKCD website for hover-over text. CC BY-NC 2.5.

There are at least three reasons why we know that the CO2 rise is due to humans, and any one of them alone is nearly dispositive. Reason the first: the CO2 record. CO2 was mostly flat for the last 2000 years at between 271 and 285 ppm, until 1750 (see Figure 4) when it starts to shoot up to the 427 ppm and rising by 2.6 ppm/year[FN10] that we see today. Moreover, in the 800,000-year ice core record, we don’t see any concentration above 300 ppm.[FN11] Countering this reasoning requires evidence that ice cores aren’t accurate records of past concentration (spoiler alert: no such evidence exists).

[INSERT IMAGE: fig4_co2_record.png]

Figure 4: Law Dome ice core plus Mauna Loa. Left panel: 154 CE to present. Right panel: 1900 to present zoom in.

Reason the second: anthropogenic emissions are about twice the increase in atmospheric concentrations (Figure 5). That means that the non-anthropogenic factors are a net sink, not a net source. Again, this points the finger at anthropogenic CO2 emissions as the cause of the rise in concentrations.[FN12]

[INSERT IMAGE: fig5_emissions_vs_increase.png]

Figure 5: Data from the Global Carbon Budget 2025. Emissions were 2.0 times the atmospheric increase over the 2015–2024 decade.

Reason the third: scientific understanding together with observations (including isotopic ones) and the carbon cycle models which express that understanding (Figure 6). CarboTope demonstrates that a physics-based model that replicates observed changes requires human emissions to be the primary source of the observed rise in concentrations AND that carbon dioxide has a very long perturbation lifetime.

[INSERT IMAGE: fig6_with_without_emissions.png]

Figure 6: CarboTope with and without human emissions. The δ13C pattern is a “fingerprint” of anthropogenic emissions.

Here is a selected list of papers and blogposts that blow right past some or all three of these reasons without (for the most part) even acknowledging their existence.

I start with two out of the five authors of the DOE Climate Working Group report (Judith Curry and Roy Spencer).

Over a period of 12 years, Judith Curry has given space on her blog at least three times to posts about humans not being primarily responsible for the growth in atmospheric concentration, and once to a post about a short carbon lifetime. These posts often came with favorable comments, never included critical evaluation, and would reach thousands of readers.

2011 and 2016, posts about presentations by Murry Salby. Curry’s commentary in 2011: “I just finished listening to Murry Salby’s podcast on Climate Change and Carbon. Wow.” And “If Salby’s analysis holds up, this could revolutionize AGW science.” Murry Salby’s fundamental problem is confusion about the ENSO relationship.

Curry did host a post by Guido van der Werf debunking Salby. Curry did say, “[t]his is exactly the sort of thing that I hoped my post would elicit,” but didn’t go so far as to offer an opinion about whether she found van der Werf convincing.

2023: guest post by Christofides et al. using a seemingly sophisticated causality analysis to “prove” that temperature comes first and CO2 second, based on an MDPI article and a Royal Society article. Curry’s commentary: “I find this analysis to be very interesting. The global carbon cycle is definitely ‘unsettled science.’” and “I think what this paper shows is that CO2 is an internal feedback in the climate system, not a forcing.” Rebuttal by And Then There’s Physics. The fundamental problem appears to be that the Christofides/Koutsoyiannis approach is confounded by the ENSO relationship.

2023, guest post by Dengler & Reid claims that CO2 has a 42-year half-life. This is the excessively simple model leading to an unrealistically short lifetime problem. They published an MDPI paper that year as well.

Over at least 4 posts and 12 years, Judith Curry has platformed these claims without ever offering a critical evaluation of them for her audience.

Meanwhile, in 2009 Roy Spencer dabbled in hardcore doubt regarding human contribution to rising CO2 concentrations, based on the ENSO confusion. He finishes his second piece with this statement: “And just how strenuous and vehement the resulting objections are to what I have presented above will be a good indication of how politicized the science of global warming has become,” not considering that strenuous and vehement objections could also result from posting really bad science. However, in contrast to Curry, Spencer did show some capability for learning — see his posts in 2019, 2022, and 2023. However, his updated approach still falls for the simplistic model problem, and therefore a perturbation lifetime that is much shorter than reality.

I continue with Essenhigh, 2009, which was the original impetus for this project. Robert Essenhigh, a mechanical engineer at OSU, decided to model the carbon cycle as a perfectly stirred reactor, found a CO2 lifetime of 5 years, and got his analysis published in Energy & Fuels, a respected chemical engineering journal.[FN13] A rebuttal to this paper was published in the same journal in 2011 by Gavin Cawley. I also wrote up a whole response to Essenhigh, highlighting among many other issues that the wildly different lifetimes for different isotopes of carbon should have been a red flag that there was something wrong in his assumptions.[FN14]

Other contrarian examples include Humlum et al. (2013), Harde (2017), Segalstad (1998), and Starr (1993). For a while, Ernst Beck’s theories that pre-1950 CO2 concentrations were highly variable were very popular on contrarian websites and managed to be published in 2007 and 2010/22.[FN15] Again, this has been repeatedly raised, submitted, sometimes published, and rebutted over and over again over more than three decades.

Some published rebuttals include Köhler et al. (2018) criticizing Harde, Meijer (2007) and Keeling (2007) criticizing Beck, Richardson (2013), Kern and Leuenberger (2013), and Masters and Benestad (2013) all criticizing Humlum.

A couple of mainstream scientist examples

I have noticed some “long-lifetime” dubiousness among scientists who work on SLCFs such as methane and black carbon. I think there are plenty of good ways to argue the importance of reductions to both pollutants (particularly because of methane’s role as an ozone precursor and black carbon’s role as a component of PM) without trying to make CO2 seem less dangerous (whether by using short GWP timespans or by believing that CO2 has a shorter lifetime than the consensus of the carbon cycle community).

In 2005, Mark Jacobson used a simple model approach to come up with a CO2 lifetime of 30–95 years as part of a paper on black carbon reductions. I don’t know if he ever used this equation again.

Stephen Schwartz from Brookhaven National Lab seems to have had a long time interest in this subject, at least as far back as a referee comment in 2012 where he took issue with the authors excluding “Simpler representations of the impulse response function of CO2 as a single exponential decay that are based on the observed rate of uptake of CO2 by the oceans and terrestrial biosphere (difference between emissions and increase of atmospheric CO2),” specifically citing the Jacobson (2005) study.

Schwartz states that “if emissions of CO2 were halted today or at some point in the near future, one could expect something like 60% of the excess CO2 and associated forcing to disappear within 100 years. (I recognize that the models tell us that the decrease of the present excess is not exactly the same as the decrease of a pulse, but I would argue that that difference is of second order because most of the excess CO2 is fairly new).” I tested this statement, and whether using FaIR or CarboTope, a century after zeroing emissions 70% of the anthropogenic increase remains in contrast to just over a third remaining from a pulse (Figure 7). Not a second order difference.

[INSERT IMAGE: fig7_stop_vs_pulse.png]

Figure 7: The difference in atmospheric response to either stopping emissions or adding a pulse in 2030. CarboTope and FaIR show 66–71% of the increase in CO2 concentrations will remain in the atmosphere 100 years after zero emissions, in contrast to about a third of a CO2 pulse.

Then in 2021, Schwartz submitted an AGU poster and a paper. The ACP paper was not published, but it was open discussion, so we can see both the preprint and the reviews. The conclusion of the paper was that “cessation of anthropogenic emissions atmospheric [sic] would result in substantial recovery of CO2 toward its preindustrial value in less than a century,” consistent with his beliefs in 2012 but inconsistent with consensus science (and CarboTope, too!).

The referee who was a carbon cycle expert[FN16] was highly critical, but the second stated that “I agree that simple box-model can answer the questions raised in this study. As I do not have expertise on ocean carbon cycle…” Someone who isn’t an ocean carbon cycle expert should perhaps be careful about agreeing that simple models can appropriately represent a complex system. This also shows the importance of actually having domain experts review papers.[FN17]

Like in Spencer’s 2009 blogpost, Schwartz preemptively rebuts his critics, stating in Appendix F to his paper that “The approach to determine the lifetime of excess CO2 based on the current budget of CO2 has drawn strenuous criticism. Those arguments are recapitulated here and rebuttals presented to forestall their being raised once again in criticism of the present study.” To give Schwartz credit, he at least recognizes the existence of these arguments, even if I don’t find his rebuttals convincing. Schwartz also has a history of lowballing other earth system properties, such as his 2007 paper estimating a climate sensitivity of 1.1 °C (revised the next year to 1.9 °C), below the likely ranges estimated by the IPCC either back then (AR4: 2–4.5 °C), or today (AR6: 2.5 to 4.0 °C).

Conclusions

I will note how annoying it is that the short lifetime/no anthropogenic contribution argument has such legs (including publications in reputable journals) despite being so clearly wrong. CarboTope is just one more piece of evidence that if you build a model based on physics and calibrate it to observed data, long carbon dioxide lifetimes are an emergent property. All the time spent in rebutting these bad arguments could be better spent investigating the many real uncertainties and challenges in understanding the complexities of the carbon cycle.

I will also note that CarboTope is a better model than any of those presented in the previous section. However, I generally assume that any disagreements between CarboTope and the consensus science are a result of flaws in CarboTope, not flaws in the consensus science. If, somehow, I persuaded myself that I’d discovered some way to improve carbon cycle modeling, I would try to team up with a scientist working in the field to test my results. Then, if they pass the laugh test, I would work with that scientist to write the results up in a way that people in the field would understand (this is what I am in the process of doing with a sea level rise emulator I’m working on).

Meanwhile, I do recognize that there are many aspects of the carbon cycle which are not intuitive, and I hope CarboTope will help people better understand this important topic.
